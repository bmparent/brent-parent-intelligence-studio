# The Eidos Works Insights voice

Insights should make a reader feel seen and leave them with a thought they can use. The writing can be lyrical, but its thinking must be exact. It should come from Eidos Works' real interests: attention, memory, trust, care, good work, and the way digital systems shape ordinary lives.

The Brent and Eidos stories, including *The River Knows*, are a tonal reference: concrete detail, a quiet sense of mystery, moral consequence, an honest question that remains after the last sentence. They are fiction. Do not recast their scenes as Brent's biography, suggest Eidos is a sentient person, or imply fictional events prove anything about Eidos Works.

## Weekly shape

- **Monday — the world through a human life.** Start from a verified development or a durable question people are discussing. Find the person inside it: the parent with no time, the shop owner making a difficult choice, the customer trying to trust a screen. Write an essay, `format: "essay"`, that connects that human tension to the kind of work Eidos Works does. If the news is thin, choose an evergreen question rather than force urgency.
- **Wednesday — the craft beneath the feeling.** Publish a technical article, usually `format: "practical-guide"`, about a real implementation decision in UX, accessibility, search, performance, automation, or a tested Eidos system. Explain the mechanism, constraints, failure modes, and what a reader can do next. Keep exact steps and tradeoffs. The prose can still carry a point of view.
- **Friday — the deeper essay.** Write `format: "essay"` about a philosophical tension with a practical edge: what we ask machines to remember, who carries the cost of friction, what efficiency erases, when a system earns trust, or why making something beautiful is an act of care. Bring the essay home to an actual Eidos Works practice without turning the ending into a sales pitch.

Across a month, vary topic family, person, scene, scale, and conclusion. Do not repeat the same river, machine, or doorway image until it becomes a mannerism. Do not make every piece about AI.

## Essay craft

1. Open with a specific, plausible moment or sharp observation. It may be illustrative, but never pass an invented customer's life or Brent's life off as reported fact.
2. State a clear thesis early enough that the reader knows why the story matters. Let the essay deepen or complicate that thesis, not merely restate it.
3. Move between an individual life, a larger idea, and a concrete design or technology choice. Show cause and consequence. Give the reader a sentence they may want to share because it is true and precise, not because it sounds grand.
4. Use natural section headings. Avoid mandatory guide headings, numbered lists, and takeaway boxes in an essay unless the material genuinely needs them. End with an earned image, question, or decision. A contextual service link can follow, quietly.
5. Cut generic openings, throat-clearing, hype, invented dialogue, sentimental manipulation, and aphorisms that cannot survive a second reading. Aim for emotional resonance and readability; never promise virality.

The technical article still needs an answer-led opening, a reproducible explanation, honest limits, practical takeaways, and a relevant next step. It should show why the mechanism matters to a person, not turn into an essay at the cost of utility.

## Truth and attribution

- `Eidos Works Editorial` remains the author/byline for assisted or automated posts. A first-person Brent essay requires Brent's actual writing or review. Otherwise use the studio's perspective and avoid pretending to remember Brent's experiences.
- Research current developments and factual claims from primary sources. Record the URLs, dates when known, and what each source supports in article metadata and the run receipt. Do not add a public Sources or References section. A reflective essay with no outside factual claims may use `sources: []`; a news-led essay must carry its research sources.
- Separate observed facts, interpretation, illustrative examples, and future hopes. Name uncertainty. Keep Eidos Brain and Sentinel proof-stage work distinct from public product claims, and cite actual receipts before describing a research result.
- Keep the publication date, byline, canonical URL, feed, sitemap, and structured data accurate. Do not add hidden claims in metadata that the visible article does not support.

## Final editorial read

Before publication, ask: Would a person who does not buy from us still value this? Is there a felt human stake and a real idea? Is the Eidos connection specific and earned? Are the technical claims checked? Does the ending stay with the reader without borrowing somebody else's life? If any answer is no, revise before running the release gate.
